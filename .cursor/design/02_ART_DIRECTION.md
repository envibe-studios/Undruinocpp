# Art Direction

Art direction is not about making the game look beautiful.

It is about making every environment, object, machine, and person feel like they belong to the same civilization.

Every artistic decision should reinforce the World Pillars.

---

# Artistic Goal

Players should immediately recognize an image from Undruinocpp without seeing a logo.

The visual identity should communicate:

- age
- ingenuity
- habitation
- history
- maintenance
- optimism through hardship

The world should feel believable because it has accumulated centuries of repairs rather than centuries of perfection.

---

# Core Principle

Nothing is new.

Nothing is disposable.

Everything has been repaired.

Everything has been modified.

Everything has a history.

---

# Layered Generations

Ships were not built once.

They have evolved continuously.

Every generation added something.

Every generation repaired something.

Every generation solved different problems.

The result is visible history.

Old systems remain even when newer ones surround them.

Technology grows instead of being replaced.

A hallway may contain equipment installed centuries apart.

This layering is desirable.

---

# The Beauty of Repair

Repairs are not flaws.

Repairs are evidence of survival.

Visible repairs should be celebrated.

Examples:

- welded seams
- reinforced brackets
- mismatched bolts
- replacement panels
- handwritten labels
- patched cables
- protective guards added decades later

Objects should look cared for.

Not abandoned.

---

# Functional Honesty

Everything should appear to have a purpose.

Avoid decorative shapes that exist only because they look futuristic.

Players should believe they understand why something was built even if they do not know exactly how it works.

If an object has moving parts...

those parts should appear necessary.

---

# Human Hands

Technology should reveal the people who maintain it.

Examples include:

- fingerprints in grease
- worn paint around handles
- polished brass from thousands of touches
- notes taped to machines
- chalk markings
- handwritten maintenance logs
- engraved names
- personal decorations

Machines are part of people's lives.

Not museum pieces.

---

# Materials

Primary materials:

- painted steel
- cast iron
- aluminum
- brass
- copper
- rubber
- wood
- leather
- cloth
- glass
- ceramic

Plastic exists but should rarely dominate a space.

Natural materials make the fleet feel inhabited.

---

# Surface Wear

Wear should tell a story.

Edges wear first.

Handles become polished.

Corners dent.

Paint fades.

Oil accumulates around moving machinery.

Rust appears where maintenance cannot completely stop time.

Dust gathers only where people rarely visit.

Never apply random weathering.

Every mark should have a believable cause.

---

# Lighting

Warm light means life.

Cold light means function.

Examples:

Crew spaces:
- tungsten
- lanterns
- fireplaces where practical
- warm incandescent bulbs
- candles

Industrial spaces:
- fluorescent tubes
- work lamps
- warning strobes

Emergency:
- red
- amber
- flashing practical lights

Darkness should feel mysterious rather than empty.

Players should always understand why light exists.

---

# Color Philosophy

Avoid the sterile white often associated with science fiction.

Primary palette:

- warm brass
- muted green
- navy
- charcoal
- dark steel
- rust
- faded cream
- worn reds
- aged copper

Bright colors should communicate function rather than decoration.

Examples:

yellow = caution

red = emergency

blue = life support

green = operational

Color should earn its place.

---

# Interfaces

Technology should be tactile.

Prefer:

- switches
- rotary knobs
- levers
- analog gauges
- CRT displays
- mechanical indicators
- physical buttons

Touchscreens should be rare.

Players should feel like they are operating machinery.

Not navigating software.

---

# Soundless Storytelling

Every room should communicate its purpose before dialogue begins.

Players should understand:

Who works here?

Who repaired this?

Who lives here?

How old is this place?

How often is it used?

What recently happened here?

Environment tells story first.

Dialogue confirms it.

---

# Civilization Over Machinery

Never forget:

The fleet is not a collection of ships.

It is humanity's home.

Every environment should contain evidence of life.

Books.

Food.

Plants where possible.

Children's drawings.

Music.

Games.

Artwork.

Prayer.

Celebration.

Grief.

Love.

People leave traces everywhere.

---

# Things To Avoid

Avoid:

- pristine science fiction
- overly minimalist interiors
- glossy white corridors
- floating holograms everywhere
- unnecessary blue lighting
- anonymous environments
- perfectly symmetrical spaces
- decorative machinery without function
- technology that appears disposable

---

# Artistic Test

Before approving an asset, environment, or prop ask:

Could only this civilization have built this?

Does this object reveal its history?

Can the player imagine the people who use it?

Would repairing this object tell a better story than replacing it?

If the answer is no...

continue iterating.

## Artistic Influences

The visual language of Undruinocpp draws inspiration from several movements.

Primary:

- Cassettepunk

Secondary:

- Industrial maritime engineering
- Solarpunk philosophy
- Art Nouveau craftsmanship
- Historic workshops and machine shops
- Early electrical engineering
- Victorian scientific instruments

These are inspirations rather than restrictions.

Every asset should feel like it belongs to the same civilization rather than to a specific artistic movement.

## Things We Are Not

Undruinocpp is NOT:

- Cyberpunk
- Clean NASA futurism
- Apple-inspired minimalism
- Military science fiction
- Corporate science fiction
- Disposable consumer technology
- Highly polished utopian futures
- Neon-drenched dystopias
