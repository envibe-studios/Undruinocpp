# Level Design

Levels exist to create memorable journeys.

A mission is not a destination.

It is the sequence of experiences that occur between departure and extraction.

Players should remember the journey more than the map.

---

# Core Principle

The world should encourage movement.

Players should rarely remain in one place for long.

Travel is not downtime.

Travel is gameplay.

Every journey should create opportunities for:

- conversation
- repair
- planning
- discovery
- danger
- wonder
- improvisation

---

# Missions Are Expeditions

Every mission should feel like leaving civilization.

The flotilla is safety.

The planet is uncertainty.

The further players travel from the fleet, the more isolated they should feel.

Returning home should feel like genuine relief.

---

# Open World With Purpose

Maps should feel open without becoming empty.

Players should have meaningful freedom of movement.

However, the scale should support missions lasting approximately 30–60 minutes.

Bigger is not better.

Density of interesting decisions is more valuable than physical size.

---

# Points of Interest

Points of Interest (POIs) are the anchors of exploration.

Each should provide a meaningful experience.

Examples include:

- objectives
- abandoned structures
- resource opportunities
- environmental storytelling
- unique encounters
- puzzles
- faction activity
- hidden discoveries

POIs should feel intentionally placed rather than randomly scattered.

---

# The Journey Between POIs

The space between destinations is as important as the destinations themselves.

Travel provides opportunities for:

- engineering repairs
- ammunition management
- crew discussion
- navigation decisions
- unexpected enemy encounters
- wildlife
- weather
- radio transmissions
- discoveries
- side objectives
- collectible lore

The world should never feel like empty terrain separating objectives.

---

# Rhythms of Tension

Every mission should naturally alternate between emotional states.

For example:

Safety

↓

Curiosity

↓

Discovery

↓

Tension

↓

Action

↓

Recovery

↓

Wonder

↓

Danger

↓

Relief

The exact sequence may vary.

Avoid maintaining maximum intensity for the entire mission.

Players need time to breathe.

---

# Environmental Storytelling

Every location should answer:

Why was this place built?

Who used it?

Why is it abandoned?

What happened here?

What can the player learn without dialogue?

The world should tell stories before NPCs do.

---

# Meaningful Exploration

Exploration should reward curiosity.

Rewards are not limited to resources.

Players may discover:

- history
- technology
- alternate routes
- faction perspectives
- hidden mechanics
- visual landmarks
- memorable moments

Wonder is a valid reward.

---

# Multiple Approaches

Whenever practical, allow players to decide:

Where to go first.

What to investigate.

When to engage.

When to retreat.

Level design should support player agency rather than rigid scripting.

---

# Living Worlds

Players should feel that the world exists independently of them.

Creatures migrate.

Factions patrol.

Weather changes.

Conflicts continue.

The player enters an existing world rather than activating it.

---

# Landmarks

Every region should have memorable landmarks.

Players should naturally orient themselves through observation.

Distinct silhouettes are more memorable than minimaps.

If players describe locations to each other, they should reference visible landmarks rather than coordinates.

---

# Emergent Encounters

Not every memorable moment should be scripted.

The best stories often arise from unexpected interactions between:

- wildlife
- enemy patrols
- environmental hazards
- player decisions
- mission objectives

Level design should encourage these interactions without requiring them.

---

# Respect the Player's Time

Every major area should justify the time required to reach it.

Travel should either:

- build anticipation,
- create interesting gameplay,
- reveal the world,
- or deepen player relationships.

If travel accomplishes none of these, reconsider the layout.

---

# Level Design Test

Before approving a level, ask:

Will players remember the journey?

Does travel create meaningful experiences?

Can memorable stories emerge naturally?

Does the world reward curiosity?

Does this level strengthen the feeling of being on an expedition?

If the answer is no, continue iterating.

# Graybox First

Every new level should prove gameplay before proving art.

Early graybox levels should answer:

- Is navigation enjoyable?
- Is travel too long or too short?
- Do encounters occur at the right frequency?
- Do POIs feel rewarding?
- Does pacing create memorable stories?
- Does the crew naturally communicate?

Art should amplify proven gameplay rather than compensate for weak gameplay.

A beautiful level with poor pacing is still a poor level.
