# Audio Philosophy

Audio is one of the primary tools used to create presence.

Players should not merely hear the world.

They should believe they are physically inside it.

Sound should communicate information, emotion, history, and life.

Silence should be used intentionally.

---

# The Ship Is Alive

The ship should never feel silent.

Even when idle.

Players should hear:

- engines breathing
- cooling fans
- relays clicking
- pipes expanding
- distant machinery
- hull vibrations
- electrical hum
- footsteps
- conversations
- doors operating

Ships are living environments.

Silence should feel unusual.

---

# Mechanical Honesty

Every machine should sound believable.

Players should understand that machinery is working even if they do not fully understand how.

Mechanical sounds should suggest:

weight

pressure

momentum

energy

wear

effort

Avoid generic science-fiction sound effects.

Prefer physical sounds that could plausibly exist.

---

# Layers of Sound

Every environment should contain multiple layers.

Examples include:

Ambient machinery

↓

Environmental sounds

↓

Crew activity

↓

Interactive machinery

↓

Warnings

↓

Combat

↓

Music

No single layer should dominate constantly.

---

# Distance Creates Space

The player should hear the world beyond the room they occupy.

Examples:

An engine room several decks away.

Crew conversations through an open doorway.

A distant alarm.

Cargo being moved.

Metal expanding.

Thunder outside.

Sound should make the world feel larger than what the player sees.

---

# Audio As Information

Players should often recognize situations before seeing them.

Examples:

A struggling engine.

An overheating system.

Incoming aircraft.

Wildlife.

Weather.

Weapon charge-up.

Hull stress.

A skilled crew should learn to diagnose problems by ear.

---

# Human Presence

Civilization should always be audible.

Examples:

Laughter.

Music.

Tools.

Cooking.

Children playing.

Footsteps.

Celebration.

Prayer.

Arguments.

Life should leave an acoustic fingerprint.

---

# Dynamic Tension

As danger increases, audio should become denser.

Not necessarily louder.

The player should subconsciously notice:

more alarms

faster machinery

more radio traffic

more overlapping voices

heavier breathing

greater urgency

As danger subsides, the ship should gradually breathe again.

---

# Music Supports, Never Dominates

Music should reinforce emotion.

It should rarely announce it.

Silence is often more powerful than orchestration.

Allow environmental audio to carry scenes whenever possible.

Music should emerge naturally from important emotional moments.

---

# Distinct Acoustic Identity

Every major location should sound different.

Players should be able to identify where they are without opening their eyes.

The bridge should sound different from engineering.

Engineering should sound different from the tavern.

The tavern should sound different from the library.

Sound is architecture.

---

# The Outside World

The wilderness should contrast sharply with civilization.

The player should immediately feel when they have left humanity behind.

Outside should sound:

older

larger

less predictable

less familiar

Civilization sounds built.

Nature sounds indifferent.

---

# Audio Creates Memory

Players often remember sounds more vividly than visuals.

Create sounds that become emotionally associated with moments.

Examples include:

The engine finally stabilizing.

The warning siren before disaster.

The satisfying click of a repaired switch.

The distant horn of the flotilla welcoming crews home.

These sounds become emotional anchors.

---

# Audio Test

When designing audio ask:

Does this make the world feel inhabited?

Can players learn from what they hear?

Does the sound reveal history?

Would the world feel emptier without it?

Does this strengthen immersion rather than distract from it?

# Civilian Audio

The flotilla should sound like a civilization rather than a military operation.

Not every sound exists to support gameplay.

Some sounds exist simply because people have built lives here.

Examples include:

- public announcements
- music
- radio personalities
- interviews
- debates
- advertisements
- children's programming
- weather reports
- sports broadcasts
- cooking shows
- educational programming

These sounds remind players that millions of people continue living their lives beyond the current mission.

# Fleet Radio

The radio is not background music.

It is a living cultural artifact.

Players may choose to listen while traveling, repairing, or exploring.

Different stations should reflect different aspects of civilization.

Examples include:

- music
- fleet news
- community announcements
- historical programming
- debates
- educational broadcasts
- interviews
- comedy
- memorial broadcasts

Listening should always feel optional.

Players who engage with Fleet Radio gradually develop a deeper understanding of humanity.

The radio should never feel like a game soundtrack.

It should feel like something real people would choose to listen to.

Fleet Radio should occasionally be imperfect.

Signals fade.

Hosts stumble over words.

Commercials repeat.

Equipment crackles.

Someone forgets to mute a microphone.

The imperfections remind players that the broadcasts are created by ordinary people, not by a game system.

Fleet Radio exists to make players believe that civilization continues beyond their expedition.

Its purpose is not to entertain the player.

Its purpose is to remind the player that millions of people are living ordinary lives while extraordinary events unfold elsewhere.
